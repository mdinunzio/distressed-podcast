## 1. Situation summary and timeline

Saks Global Enterprises LLC ("Saks Global", "SGE") is the private
holding company formed in late 2024 to combine Saks Fifth Avenue, Saks
OFF 5TH, Neiman Marcus and Bergdorf Goodman [S1][S2]. It financed the
$2.7 billion Neiman Marcus acquisition largely with $2.2 billion of 11%
senior secured notes due 2029 and an asset-based revolver [S1][S2]. Within
a year it had stopped paying vendors on time, vendors stopped shipping,
sales fell, and it did a non-pro-rata exchange that split the notes into
three payment tiers and raised $600 million of new money [S4][S5][S6]. That
bought five months. It skipped a coupon on December 31, 2025 and filed
Chapter 11 in Houston on January 13-14, 2026 with a $2.6 billion
delayed-draw DIP from an ad hoc bondholder group and a $1.5 billion ABL
DIP [S8][S9][S11]. A plan converting the DIP into the equity of the
reorganized company was confirmed June 5, 2026 and went effective June 26,
2026; the company emerged as Exemplar Luxury Group with about $840
million of debt versus $3.4 billion of prepetition funded debt [S20][S22]
[S23]. Saks OFF 5TH and Last Call were wound down under a separate
liquidating plan [S20][S16].

## 2. Business overview and why it broke

**What it is.** Luxury department stores: Saks Fifth Avenue, Neiman
Marcus, Bergdorf Goodman (full price) and, until 2026, Saks OFF 5TH and
Last Call (off price) [S1]. Revenue is merchandise sales through stores
and e-commerce; the working-capital engine is inventory bought from
fashion houses on trade terms, so vendor confidence is the business.

**Moyer-style causes of distress.** Three of Moyer's causes stack:

1. *Excessive leverage from an acquisition.* HBC financed the $2.7
   billion Neiman Marcus purchase mainly with $2.2 billion of 11% notes
   [S1][S2]. At 11%, that is roughly $242 million of cash interest a year
   on the notes alone before the ABL, against a business that reported an
   adjusted EBITDA *loss* of $77 million in the second quarter of fiscal
   2025 [S26].
2. *Operating deterioration.* Consolidated revenue for the fiscal year
   ended February 1, 2025 fell 13.6% year on year [S10] (docket, via
   search excerpt); Q2 FY2025 revenue fell 13.1% to $1.6 billion and net
   loss was $288 million [S26].
3. *A working-capital spiral.* The company was already 18 months behind
   on vendor bills in February 2025 [S3]. Vendors withheld goods; the
   company received more than $550 million less inventory than forecast
   in the second half of 2025 [S10]; less inventory meant less revenue,
   which meant less ABL borrowing base and less cash to pay vendors. This
   is the retailer death loop, and it is a liquidity problem that becomes
   a solvency problem.

**Aggravating factors (press).** The former Canadian parent, Hudson's
Bay, collapsed into CCAA in March 2025 and liquidated [S25], removing any
parent support and licensing revenue. Amazon, a $475 million equity
investor, held a commercial agreement with up to $900 million of minimum
referral-fee true-ups over eight years, which it said was backed by the
Fifth Avenue flagship [S17][S27].

**Sequence.** Acquisition (Dec 2024) → vendor arrears and sales decline
(H1 2025) → advisers hired (May 2025) → $600 million new money via a
tiered exchange (Jun-Aug 2025) → inventory shortfall through holiday
(H2 2025) → missed coupon (Dec 31, 2025) → Chapter 11 (Jan 2026) →
DIP-to-equity plan (Jun 2026).

**Creditor's caveats.**

- Adjusted EBITDA was *negative* in the last quarter we could source, so
  leverage multiples are meaningless; the relevant ratios are cash
  interest versus liquidity, and inventory versus the ABL borrowing base.
  11% on ~$2.64 billion of notes (SPV + 2O + 3O) is about $290 million of
  annual interest; the $121 million semi-annual coupon flagged by Octus
  and the ">$100 million" skipped on Dec 31 are consistent with that
  [S7][S6a].
- "Adjusted" EBITDA add-backs were not sourced. Treat any adjusted figure
  with the usual scepticism about one-time integration costs.
- Retail leases are large fixed obligations that sit outside funded debt;
  store closures under section 365 rejection are part of the fix. Lease
  totals were not sourced.
- The ABL's minimum excess availability covenant ($200 million rising to

  the borrowing base minus drawings minus the reserve, not the facility

- Vendor payables are a form of financing. An 18-month backlog [S3] is
  effectively an unsecured loan from suppliers, and unwinding it

**Structural points.**

- *SGUS as SPV issuer.* The new money in 2025 was raised at a
  subsidiary, SGUS, which lent the proceeds to the operating company
  under intercompany loans [S10]. The SPV noteholders therefore hold a
  claim against SGUS, and SGUS holds an intercompany claim against SGE:
  a two-layer claim on the same cash flows, which is what "first out"
  practically meant here, and what the DIP was later routed through
  (the $2.6 billion DIP was made *to SGUS*, which lent $1.75 billion on
  to SGE [S12][S20]).
- *Payment waterfall inside one lien.* SPV → 2O → 3O is a
  first-out/second-out/third-out structure: the same collateral, but the
  intercreditor pays tiers in order. The 2O and 3O notes were issued by
  SGE with a first lien on Notes/Term Priority Collateral and a second
  lien on ABL Priority Collateral [S10].
- *Split collateral (ABL vs. term).* The ABL has first claim on inventory
  and receivables, the notes on everything else (brand IP, owned real
  estate that sits inside the debtors, equity of subsidiaries). This is
  the standard retail "split lien" and matters because a retailer's most
  liquid collateral belongs to the ABL.
- *Real estate outside the box.* Saks Fifth Avenue HoldCo II is the parent
  of Saks Flagship Real Property LLC, a **non-debtor** that owns 611 Fifth
  Avenue, and of 12 East 49th Street LLC, a debtor ground lessee [S27].
  Amazon and Axonic are creditors at the HoldCo II level; Amazon claims
  consent rights under the HoldCo II LLC agreement and argued the
  flagship already backed its $900 million fee true-ups [S14][S27]. The
  flagship was appraised at $3.62-3.7 billion for a mortgage financing
  (press) [S27]. Whether a mortgage was outstanding at the petition date
  was not sourced.

## 6. Where the fulcrum security sits and why

Moyer's fulcrum is the most senior class that is impaired and receives
the equity. Here the answer changed twice.

- *Before the exchange (H1 2025)*: one class of $2.2 billion secured
  notes sat under a small ABL. Press estimated bondholders faced nearly
  $1 billion of losses by May 2025 [S7a], so the notes were the fulcrum
  and equity was out of the money.
- *After the exchange (Aug 2025)*: the same holders now sat in three
  tiers. By December 2025 the 2O notes traded at ~12 cents [S7a], which
  says the market saw value running out inside the SPV/first-out layer:
  the fulcrum had moved up to the SPV Notes, and the 2O and 3O were
  effectively unsecured-like claims.
- *In Chapter 11*: the DIP term loan primed and rolled up the SPV Notes
  ($808 million second-out DIP) and bought participations in the 2O
  notes ($751 million third-out DIP), so the DIP itself became the
  fulcrum. The plan's "DIP Conversion" hands the equity to consenting DIP
  term loan lenders [S19][S20]; the first-out $1.0 billion new money is
  treated with take-back debt and the group also provided the $500
  million exit term loan [S12][S23].

The math: ~$3.4 billion of funded claims plus >$600 million of trade
claims paid in the case, against a business with negative EBITDA and
$840 million of debt at emergence [S23]. Value ran out well inside the
secured notes; general unsecured creditors other than critical vendors
got a litigation trust.

**Not sourced:** the disclosure statement's enterprise valuation and
liquidation analysis, and recoveries by class.

## 7. The restructuring, step by step

**Step 1: the out-of-court exchange (Jun-Aug 2025)** [S4][S5][S10]
1. June 27, 2025: SGUS (SPV) issues $300 million SPV Notes for cash to
   existing holders; proceeds on-lent to opco. ABL/FILO intercreditor
   signed.
2. July 21: exchange offer for any and all $2.2 billion Old Notes into a
   mix of SPV Notes, 2O Notes and 3O Notes, with a consent solicitation
   for "Proposed Amendments"; ~91.9% pre-committed.
3. August 8: settlement. $300 million more cash SPV Notes; $162.5 million
   SPV Notes issued in exchange; $1,439.2 million 2O; $440.7 million 3O;
   ~98% tendered; ~$115 million discount captured. Total new money $600
   million; total SPV Notes $762.5 million.
   *Non-pro-rata:* different holders got different mixes of tiers, which
   is why it is described as a non-pro-rata liability management
   exercise [S19a].

**Step 2: default and filing (Dec 2025-Jan 2026)** [S7][S8][S9]
4. December 31: coupon skipped; 30-day grace.
5. January 13-14: petitions for 113 debtors in SDTX; $1.75 billion
   committed package announced.

**Step 3: the DIP (Jan-Feb 2026)** [S12][S13][S15][S20]
6. $2.6 billion DIP to SGUS: $1.0 billion new money first-out (three
   draws), $808 million roll-up of SPV Notes into second-out DIP, $751
   million third-out DIP used to buy participations in 2O notes held by
   the DIP lenders. PIK interest and 6%/2%/8% premiums.
7. SGUS on-lends via a $1.75 billion DIP OpCo facility ($1.0 billion new
   plus ~$752 million intercompany roll-up).
8. $1.5 billion ABL DIP with creeping roll-up; ~$240 million incremental.
9. Interim order January 14 over Amazon/Axonic objections; final order in
   February after amendments; $330 million to vendors.

**Step 4: operational triage (Jan-Apr 2026)** [S16]
10. Close 57 of 69 Saks OFF 5TH stores, all 5 Last Call, 8 Saks Fifth
    Avenue, 1 Neiman Marcus, 14 Fifth Avenue Club locations.

**Step 5: RSA, plan, confirmation, emergence (Apr-Jun 2026)** [S18]-[S23]
11. April 1 RSA; April 5 plan and DS; $500 million exit commitment.
12. Committee settlement: >$611 million of vendor claims paid; $20
    million litigation trust with the $20 million / $80 million waterfall.
13. June 5 confirmation of both plans. June 26 effective date: DIP
    Conversion gives consenting DIP lenders the equity; $500 million PIK
    exit term loan; $340 million ABL from Bank of America; renamed
    Exemplar Luxury Group; Pentwater and Bracebridge two board seats each.

**Priming and dilution mechanics.** The 2025 exchange primed
non-participating holders by inserting a new first-out layer (SPV) above
them and by moving them into 2O/3O tiers. The DIP then primed the SPV
Notes with $1.0 billion of new-money first-out DIP, and rolled the SPV
Notes into second-out DIP. The DIP premiums (6% structuring, 2%
commitment, 8% backstop, all PIK) accrued to backstop parties and so
shifted value from non-backstop holders to the group [S12]. Exact
percentages of the new equity were not sourced.

## 9. How a special situations desk would look at each part of the structure

- **ABL.** Money-good, borrowing-base protected, rolled up in the DIP and
  re-lent at exit. A desk does not trade it; it watches excess
  availability as the liquidity clock. The $200 million → $500 million
  covenant change [S12] is the tell that lenders wanted a bigger cushion.
- **SPV Notes (first-out).** The "new money gets priority" instrument.
  The trade in mid-2025 was to participate: put in $600 million of cash
  at par and move your existing paper up the waterfall. Holding SPV
  Notes into the filing meant being rolled into second-out DIP dollar
  for dollar; being in the ad hoc group meant also writing the first-out
  DIP with SOFR + 11% PIK and 16 points of premiums [S12]. The asymmetry
  a desk sees: whoever controls the new money controls the process and
  the equity. Pentwater's ~$1.2 billion position [S29] is the control
  stake.
- **2O Notes.** At ~12 cents in December 2025 [S7a] the market said these
  were option value on a higher valuation or a litigation outcome. Note
  the DIP's third-out tranche bought $751 million of *participations* in
  2O notes held by DIP lenders [S12], a way to give the group's own 2O
  exposure a DIP-level claim; a desk would ask whether outside 2O holders
  got the same, and the plan needed "amendments" [S19]. *Not sourced:*
  final 2O recovery.
- **3O Notes.** The bottom of a secured stack that ran out of value:
  functionally unsecured. A desk would treat them as a litigation and
  releases play, not a recovery play.
- **Trade claims.** The best unsecured position in the case: critical
  vendor status meant par recoveries on >$600 million [S21], because the
  business cannot reopen without the fashion houses. Buying trade claims
  from small vendors at a discount ahead of critical-vendor approval is
  the classic desk trade; whether it happened here was not sourced.
- **HoldCo II / flagship.** A real-estate box with a $3.6-3.7 billion
  appraisal [S27] and a non-debtor owner. Amazon's argument that pledging
  it to the DIP violated its consent rights [S27] is the "where is the
  value and who controls the entity" question. The DIP got the pledge
  anyway, then Amazon settled [S15]. A desk would want the LLC agreement
  and any mortgage; neither was sourced.
- **Equity.** Zero. The lesson is that $475 million of strategic equity
  from Amazon [S17] bought no seat at the table once the notes were
  impaired.

## 10. Risks and what could derail it

The plan is effective, so the in-case risks have resolved. What remains:

- *Operating risk.* Negative EBITDA going in [S26]; 49 stores; the same
  vendors who withheld goods. The PIK exit term loan defers cash interest
  for two years [S23], which is the runway.
- *Litigation trust.* Retained causes of action against pre-filing
  parties are preserved [S28]; outcomes unknown.
- *Amazon.* Threatened "drastic" action in January [S17]; the partnership
  was reported to be exited [S27]. Any surviving claim under the $900
  million commercial agreement was not sourced.
- *Real estate.* The flagship sits in a non-debtor owner; any mortgage
  and its terms were not sourced.
- *Precedent risk for the 2025 exchange.* Non-pro-rata exchanges and
  SPV-priority structures have been litigated elsewhere (not in this
  research). The consent solicitation reached ~98%, which limits holdout
  claims here [S5].

What would have derailed it during the case: a failed DIP (the court
said the alternative was liquidation [S13]); a sustained Amazon objection
on HoldCo II; a committee fight over releases; vendors refusing to ship
even with critical-vendor payments.

## 11. Concepts to teach in this episode

1. **Causes of distress: acquisition leverage meets a working-capital
   spiral.** Why: 11% on $2.2 billion into a negative-EBITDA retailer,
   and vendors as involuntary lenders. Fact: 18-month vendor backlog
   [S3]; >$550 million inventory shortfall [S10]; Q2 adjusted EBITDA -$77
   million [S26]. Moyer: causes of financial distress; liquidity versus
   solvency.
2. **ABL versus term collateral and the intercreditor.** Why: a
   retailer's liquid collateral belongs to the ABL, and excess
   availability is the liquidity clock. Fact: $442 million ABL, 2O Notes
   with first lien on Notes Priority and second on ABL Priority
   collateral [S10]; covenant $200 → $500 million [S12]. Moyer: the
   capital structure and where value breaks (secured claims and
   collateral).
3. **Liability management: the non-pro-rata, tiered exchange.** Why: this
   is how the company bought five months without a filing, and it is the
   modern out-of-court tool Moyer's edition predates. Fact: SPV issuer,
   $600 million new money, first-out/second-out/third-out at the same
   11% coupon, ~98% consents, ~$115 million discount captured [S4][S5].
   Moyer: out-of-court restructurings and exchange offers, extended to
   uptiers and drop-downs (not in Moyer by name).
4. **DIP financing with roll-ups, priming and PIK premiums.** Why: the
   DIP decided who owns the company. Fact: $2.6 billion DIP, $1.0
   billion new money, $808 million roll-up, $751 million participations,
   SOFR + 11% PIK, 6%/2%/8% premiums; court found it the only actionable
   financing [S12][S13]. Moyer: the Chapter 11 process, DIP financing,
   adequate protection.
5. **The fulcrum security and DIP-to-equity plans.** Why: the fulcrum
   moved from the old notes to the SPV Notes to the DIP. Fact: 2O at 12
   cents [S7a]; DIP Conversion to equity [S20]; $840 million debt at
   emergence versus $3.4 billion [S23]. Moyer: valuation and the fulcrum
   security; the plan of reorganization.
6. **Structural subordination and value outside the debtor.** Why: the
   flagship is owned by a non-debtor under HoldCo II, and Amazon's
   consent rights lived there. Fact: 611 Fifth Avenue, $3.6-3.7 billion
   appraisal, Amazon/Axonic objection [S27]. Moyer: structural versus
   contractual subordination; guarantees.

Supporting concept (fold into 4 or 5): **critical vendors and the
unsecured creditors committee**: >$600 million paid to vendors while
other unsecureds got a $20 million litigation trust [S21][S28]. Moyer:
treatment of unsecured claims in the plan.

## 12. Glossary

- **ABL (asset-based loan):** a revolver whose availability is a
  percentage of eligible inventory and receivables, the "borrowing base".
- **ABL Priority Collateral / Notes (Term) Priority Collateral:** the two
  collateral pools in a split-lien retail structure; the ABL is first on
  the former, the notes on the latter, each second on the other.
- **Ad hoc group:** an informal group of holders of one or more
  instruments that organizes to negotiate together.
- **Adequate protection:** what a secured creditor gets when its
  collateral is used or primed in Chapter 11.
- **Backstop premium:** a fee paid to parties who commit to fund a
  financing whether or not others participate.
- **Chapter 11:** the US reorganization chapter; the debtor keeps
  operating under court supervision and proposes a plan.
- **Consent solicitation:** a vote to amend an indenture, here run
  alongside the exchange to permit the new structure.
- **Creeping roll-up:** a DIP feature under which prepetition revolver
  drawings are repaid with DIP borrowings over time, converting old
  claims into DIP claims.
- **Critical vendor:** a supplier the debtor may pay prepetition claims
  to, with court approval, because the business cannot run without it.
- **DIP (debtor-in-possession) financing:** post-petition loans with
  superpriority and usually priming liens.
- **DIP Conversion:** the plan mechanic by which DIP claims are exchanged
  for the reorganized equity.
- **Disclosure statement:** the court-approved document that gives voting
  creditors the information (valuation, recoveries) behind the plan.
- **Excess availability:** borrowing base minus drawings minus reserves;
  ABL covenants are usually set on it.
- **Exit financing:** debt or equity raised to fund the company on the
  effective date.
- **FILO (first-in, last-out):** an ABL tranche that is drawn first and
  repaid last, so it ranks behind the revolver in the ABL collateral.
- **First-out / second-out / third-out:** a payment waterfall among
  lenders sharing one lien: tiers are paid in order from the same
  collateral.
- **Fulcrum security:** the class where enterprise value runs out; it is
  impaired and typically receives the reorganized equity.
- **Intercreditor agreement:** the contract among lender groups that
  sets lien priority, payment order and enforcement rights.
- **Liability management exercise (LME):** an out-of-court transaction
  (exchange, uptier, drop-down) that changes priorities or raises new
  money without a filing.
- **Litigation trust:** a plan vehicle that receives estate causes of
  action and pursues them for a class of beneficiaries.
- **Non-pro-rata:** an exchange or financing in which participating
  holders receive better terms than non-participants.
- **PIK (payment in kind):** interest paid by increasing principal
  rather than in cash.
- **Prepack / prearranged:** plans negotiated before filing; here the RSA
  came during the case, so the case was neither.
- **Priming:** placing a new lien ahead of an existing lien on the same
  collateral.
- **Roll-up:** converting prepetition claims into DIP claims, usually
  dollar for dollar, so they gain DIP priority.
- **RSA (restructuring support agreement):** a contract in which
  creditors agree to support a plan with specified terms and
  milestones.
- **SPV (special purpose vehicle):** a subsidiary created to issue debt
  or hold assets separately from the parent, here SGUS.
- **Structural subordination:** a claim at a parent is paid only from
  what is left after the subsidiary's own creditors; assets in a
  non-debtor subsidiary are outside the estate.
- **Take-back debt:** new debt issued under a plan to existing lenders
  in partial satisfaction of their claims.
- **Term SOFR:** the floating-rate benchmark that replaced LIBOR.
- **Unsecured creditors committee (UCC):** the court-appointed body
  representing general unsecured creditors.
- **363 sale:** a sale of assets under section 363 of the Bankruptcy
  Code, outside a plan.
