## 1. Situation summary and timeline

Saks Global Enterprises LLC ("SGE") is the private holding company that
since December 2024 has owned Saks Fifth Avenue, Saks OFF 5TH, Neiman
Marcus, Bergdorf Goodman, Last Call and Horchow, "the largest multi-brand
luxury retailer in the world" with about 70 full-line stores and 8.4
million square feet of owned or ground-leased U.S. real estate [S1 ¶¶8-9].
It bought Neiman Marcus for a $2.7 billion enterprise value with $2.2
billion of 11% secured notes, a $1.8 billion ABL, $275 million of seller
paper and $1.544 billion of equity [S1 ¶¶36, 77]. It never had the
liquidity to pay vendors on time; vendors withheld goods; revenue fell
13.6% in the fiscal year ended February 1, 2025 [S1 ¶11]. A summer 2025
exchange raised $600 million of new money through a special-purpose
subsidiary and split the notes into first-, second-, third- and fourth-out
tiers [S1 ¶¶80-82]. It did not work: the company received more than $550
million less inventory than forecast in the second half of 2025, the ABL's
minimum-availability covenant stepped up from $200 million to $500
million in December, about $126 million of interest came due at year-end
that it could not pay, and it filed on January 13-14, 2026 [S1 ¶¶12,
86]. An ad hoc group of the new-money bondholders funded a $2.6 billion
DIP ($1 billion new money, the rest roll-ups and participations) [S1
¶18], signed an RSA on April 1, and took the company through a plan
confirmed June 5 and effective June 26, 2026 [S8]. The reorganized
company, renamed Exemplar Luxury Group, emerged with a $340 million ABL,
a $500 million payment-in-kind exit term loan and about $1.35 billion of
take-back preferred units, owned by the DIP lenders led by Pentwater
Capital and Bracebridge Capital [S7][S24][S25] (press for the ABL and
exit loan figures). Old equity, including Amazon's $475 million, received
nothing.

## 2. Business overview and why it broke

**What it is.** Full-price luxury department stores (33 Saks Fifth
Avenue, 36 Neiman Marcus, 2 Bergdorf Goodman at the petition date), the
off-price chains Saks OFF 5TH (81 stores) and Last Call (5), and five
e-commerce sites [S1 ¶¶23-33, 42]. Revenue is (i) owned merchandise
bought wholesale, (ii) consigned merchandise, (iii) concession
("shop-in-shop") revenue share and (iv) drop-ship e-commerce [S1 ¶49].
The top 3% of customers, spending more than $10,000 a year, generate about
40% of gross merchandise value, with retention above 90% [S1 ¶88]. About
14,610 full-time and 2,220 part-time employees [S1 ¶51].

**The structure a creditor must draw before anything else.**
- The Saks Fifth Avenue flagship at 611 Fifth Avenue is owned by
  *non-debtor* Saks Flagship Real Property LLC under a $1.25 billion
  securitized mortgage, and ground-leased to debtor 12 East 49th Street
  LLC. Both sit under Saks Fifth Avenue HoldCo II LLC [S1 ¶39, chart].
- 31 other properties sit in the HBS JV with Simon Property Group (Saks
  owns 62.4%), leased back on 20-year portfolio leases, with a $428.1
  million securitized loan on the JV's interests [S1 ¶38].
- The brand intellectual property for Saks, Saks Fifth Avenue, OFF 5TH,
  Neiman Marcus and Bergdorf Goodman was transferred to subsidiaries
  "that are not guarantors on the Debtors' funded debt" and is licensed
  to a 50/50 joint venture with Authentic Brands (Authentic Luxury
  Group), whose equity is not pledged [S1 ¶¶46-47, fn.11].
- Saks OFF 5TH's e-commerce business ("SO5 Digital") has been a
  standalone credit group since 2021, ~80% owned by Saks Global and the
  rest by Insight Partners, with its own $20 million term loan
  (Callodine) and a $125 million ABL (Citibank) that was paid off on
  January 6, 2026 [S1 ¶¶26, 73-76].

So the two assets a generalist would call "the collateral", the flagship
and the brands, are largely outside the operating company's lenders'
reach. The notes are secured by the operating subsidiaries' assets other
than what the ABL takes first.

**Moyer-style causes of distress.**
1. *Acquisition leverage.* $2.2 billion at 11% is about $242 million of
   cash interest a year, before the ABL and before the 11% on the $600
   million of new money added in 2025 (the company paid $130 million of
   interest in June 2025 and owed about $126 million at December 31)
   [S1 ¶¶36, 84, 86].
2. *Operating deterioration with a specific, non-secular cause.* The
   declaration insists demand was not the problem: concession sales were
   up 2% to 18% every month of fiscal 2025 and "when stores have
   inventory, the inventory sells" [S1 ¶¶87-88]. Owned-inventory flow
   was the problem, and it was caused by liquidity.
3. *The working-capital spiral.* Aged payables → vendors ship less →
   inventory 9% below prior year at the end of Q2 [S1 ¶84] → borrowing
   base shrinks → less cash → more aged payables. The ABL agent added
   more than $50 million of discretionary reserves during 2025 and the
   minimum excess availability covenant tripled in December [S1 ¶¶12,
   86]. Add a one-time merchandising system integration failure at
   Neiman Marcus and Bergdorf in August [S1 ¶83] and a seller note
   maturing February 3, 2026 [S1 ¶53].
4. *Parent collapse.* HBC, which had financed Saks stores as part of its
   own credit group until 2024, filed CCAA in March 2025 and liquidated
   [S1 ¶35].

**Sequence.** Acquisition (Dec 2024) → vendor arrears (early 2025) → SLR
financing fails (Apr-May 2025) → $600 million tiered exchange (Jun-Aug
2025) → only $244 million reaches vendors → holiday inventory shortfall
(H2 2025) → ABL covenant step-ups and account control (Dec 2025) → missed
interest (Dec 31) → no out-of-court deal with equity holders or third
parties [S1 ¶16] → Chapter 11 with the ad hoc group's DIP (Jan 2026) →
RSA (Apr 1) → confirmation (Jun 5) → emergence (Jun 26).

**Creditor's caveats.**
- EBITDA was negative through the filing, so leverage multiples are
  meaningless; the binding constraints were cash interest (roughly $290
  million a year on ~$2.64 billion of 11% notes) against ABL availability.
- Availability, not the facility size, is the liquidity number: $1.8
  billion of commitments, $442 million drawn, but a borrowing base tied
  to shrinking inventory, more than $50 million of agent reserves and a
  $500 million minimum-availability floor [S1 ¶¶58-59, 86].
- Trade payables are financing: the declaration describes "an
  unsustainable level of aged trade payables" [S1 ¶11]; the critical
  vendor motion sought ~$337 million on day one (press) and the plan
  ultimately committed more than $600 million to vendors [S16][S17].
- Two CMBS loans totaling $1.68 billion are excluded from the $3.4
  billion because they sit at non-debtors or the JV [S1 fn.12]; the
  projections still carry the $1.25 billion flagship mortgage on the
  consolidated balance sheet [S6 Ex.C].
- The projections exclude February-May 2026 and assume no litigation
  trust receipts, $150 million minimum excess availability through
  FY2027, and no fresh-start adjustments [S6 Ex.C].
- "Adjusted EBITDA" in the projections adds back non-recurring items,
  Chapter 11 costs and non-cash occupancy expense [S6 Ex.C]; FY2027
  reported EBITDA is $325 million against adjusted $381 million.

**How the tiers actually work.** Four agreements govern priority [S1
¶¶68-71]: the ABL/Notes Intercreditor (Second Amended and Restated, Aug
8, 2025) splits collateral between the ABL side (ABL plus FILO on-loan)
and the notes side (Opco Notes plus NPC on-loan); the ABL/FILO
Intercreditor (Jun 27, 2025) puts the FILO behind the ABL on ABL
collateral; the Equal Priority Intercreditor (Aug 8, 2025) makes the NPC
on-loan and all Opco Notes share one lien on notes collateral; and the
Payment Administration Agreement (Aug 8, 2025) then orders *payment*
within that shared lien: FILO first-out, NPC "additional first out",
then 2O, then 3O, then the Old Notes "fourth out". The SPV Notes sit one
level up: SGUS lends the proceeds down as the FILO and NPC on-loans, so
an SPV noteholder's claim is on SGUS, whose asset is a first-out claim on
SGE, plus a $200 million guarantee from HoldCo II [S1 ¶60]. That is the
whole trick of the 2025 deal: new money became first-out without a new
lien, by owning the top of the payment waterfall through an SPV.

**The HoldCo II box.** Saks Fifth Avenue HoldCo II LLC owns the equity of
the non-debtor flagship owner and of the debtor ground lessee. It
guaranteed the SPV Notes up to $200 million [S1 ¶60], guaranteed Axonic's
put arrangements and pledged the equity of 12 East 49th Street LLC to
Axonic [S1 ¶43]. The interim DIP order then gave the new-money DIP a
first-priority pledge of the equity of Saks Flagship Real Property LLC
[S3 term sheet]. Amazon's objection at the first-day hearing was about
exactly this box (press) [S13].

## 6. Where the fulcrum security sits and why

Moyer's fulcrum is the most senior impaired class, the one that receives
the reorganized equity. Here it moved three times, and the plan puts a
number on it.

- *Pre-exchange (H1 2025)*: one $2.2 billion class of secured notes under
  a small ABL; press put bondholder losses near $1 billion by May 2025
  [S14]; equity out of the money.
- *Post-exchange (Aug 2025)*: the same holders sat in a waterfall.
  Twelve-cent quotes on the 2O Notes in December [S14] said value ran
  out inside the first-out layer; the fulcrum had moved up into the SPV
  Notes / on-loans.
- *In Chapter 11*: the DIP rolled the SPV Notes into second-out DIP
  ($763 million allowed) and bought $751 million of 2O participations
  into third-out DIP ($730 million allowed) [S6 pp.12-13]. The plan
  hands the "DIP Equity Distribution" to Classes 3-B and 3-C [S6 p.23].
  The DIP is the fulcrum.

The arithmetic at the $3.0 billion midpoint: DIP ABL ~$714 million paid
in cash from exit facilities; first-out DIP $1.267 billion paid 100% in
take-back paper (the plan supplement shows $1.354 billion face of
Take-Back Preferred Units); that leaves roughly $1 billion of value for
the second- and third-out DIP (~$1.49 billion of claims) as common
equity, and nothing but litigation-trust interests for the $1.51 billion
of 2O, $462 million of 3O, $54 million of Old Notes and $1.5-1.8 billion
of general unsecured claims [S6 pp.11-17][S7]. The 2O Notes are the
class whose 12-cent price the market got right.

## 9. How a special situations desk would look at each part of the structure

- **ABL.** Money-good and in control of the clock. Watch three numbers:
  drawn amount, agent reserves, and the minimum excess availability
  covenant. The December step-up to $500 million and the deposit-account
  control notices [S1 ¶¶12, 86] were the trigger a desk would have seen
  coming from the covenant schedule.
- **SPV Notes / on-loans.** The instrument to own in 2025 if you were
  going to write the new money, because it bought the top of the
  payment waterfall and a HoldCo II guarantee. Holding it *without*
  joining the DIP left you in Class 4-A at 0.11% [S6 p.13]. The lesson
  is that in a modern LME the security is the ticket to the next round,
  not the recovery.
- **2O Notes.** At 12 cents in December [S14], the market priced them as
  an option on litigation. The third-out DIP gave the group's own 2O
  paper a DIP claim through $751 million of participations [S1 ¶18]; the
  plan then routed all other 2O holders to Trust A-2, behind Trust A-1
  [S6 p.112]. A desk that bought 2O at 12 cents outside the group owns a
  lawsuit, not a company.
- **3O and Old Notes.** Tiers three and four of a waterfall that stopped
  at tier one. Trust A-3 and A-4.
- **Trade claims.** The best unsecured seat: critical vendors paid in
  full (press), go-forward trade a 2.78% cash pool plus Trust B [S6
  p.16]. Buying small vendors' receivables ahead of critical-vendor
  status is the classic trade; the DS's Class 4-D/4-E split is the
  outcome the trade depends on.
- **HoldCo II / flagship.** A $1.25 billion mortgage on a building that
  is not in the estate [S1 ¶39], a $200 million guarantee of the SPV
  Notes, Axonic's pledge, Amazon's consent rights, and a DIP pledge of
  the owner's equity [S1 ¶¶43, 60][S3]. The final order's marshaling
  clause, which makes the DIP recover from everything else first for 150
  days [S4], is what the objections bought. A desk maps this box before
  pricing any instrument.
- **Brand IP.** Held by non-guarantor subsidiaries and licensed to a JV
  with Authentic Brands whose equity is unpledged [S1 ¶¶46-47]. Not in
  the notes' collateral. That is why "senior secured" here means secured
  by stores and subsidiaries' equity, not by the names on the door.
- **Equity.** $1.544 billion contributed in December 2024 [S1 ¶77], zero
  eighteen months later.

## 10. Risks and what could derail it

The plan is effective. Remaining risks:
- *Operating.* Negative EBITDA through 2025 [S1 ¶87]; projections need
  adjusted EBITDA to go from $218 million (partial FY2026) to $381
  million in FY2027 with revenue of $5.9 billion [S6 Ex.C]; the same
  vendors; a $150 million minimum-availability floor.
- *Capital structure.* $500 million exit term loan paying in kind
  (press), $1.354 billion of take-back preferred with a 1.25x minimum
  MOIC ahead of the common [S7], and the $1.25 billion flagship CMBS
  still on the consolidated balance sheet [S6 Ex.C].
- *Litigation trust.* Retained causes of action from the special
  committee's investigation (16 witness interviews; Milbank and A&M)
  [S6 pp.52-53]; outcomes unknown; the company recoups first.
- *Simon lift-stay* over terminated leases, undecided as of the DS [S6
  p.49].
- *Axonic* settlement or litigation over the put and the HoldCo II
  pledge [S1 ¶¶43-45][S6 p.19].
- *Precedent.* The 2025 tiered exchange and the DIP participations are
  the kind of structures being litigated elsewhere; ~98% consents limit
  holdout claims here [S19].

What would have derailed it in the case: a failed DIP (the court found
no other credit available [S4 ¶(3)]); a sustained HoldCo II fight; a
committee challenge to the roll-ups before April 15, 2026 [S4]; vendors
refusing to ship.

## 11. Concepts to teach in this episode

1. **Causes of distress: acquisition leverage meets a working-capital
   spiral.** Why: $242 million of note interest on a negative-EBITDA
   retailer whose suppliers were its lenders. Fact: revenue -13.6% [S1
   ¶11], >$550 million inventory shortfall, $126 million interest unpaid
   [S1 ¶86], "when stores have inventory, the inventory sells" [S1 ¶87].
   Moyer: causes of financial distress; liquidity versus solvency.
2. **Split collateral and the ABL as liquidity governor.** Why: the
   lender with the smallest claim controlled the cash. Fact: $442
   million drawn on $1.8 billion; covenant $200m → $500m; agent reserves
   >$50 million; account control notices [S1 ¶¶12, 58-59, 86]. Moyer:
   secured claims and collateral; where value breaks.
3. **Liability management: first-out / second-out / third-out through an
   SPV.** Why: this is how the company bought five months, and how the
   later winners got to the front. Fact: $300 million + $300 million SPV
   Notes, on-loans, four intercreditor agreements, ~98% consents, $115
   million discount, only $244 million to vendors [S1 ¶¶60-71, 80-82].
   Moyer: exchange offers and out-of-court restructuring, extended to
   modern uptier and SPV structures (not in Moyer by name).
4. **DIP financing: new money, roll-ups, participations, PIK premiums,
   milestones.** Why: the DIP decided who owned the company. Fact: $2.6
   billion, $1 billion new; SOFR + 11% PIK; 2%/8%/10% premiums; $808
   million roll-up; $751 million participations; 160-day milestone
   schedule; court finding of no alternative [S1 ¶18][S2][S3][S4].
   Moyer: the Chapter 11 process, DIP financing, adequate protection.
5. **The fulcrum security and DIP-to-equity plans.** Why: the fulcrum
   moved from the old notes to the SPV layer to the DIP. Fact: PJT TEV
   $2.5-3.5 billion; first-out 100%, second/third-out equity, SPV stub
   0.11%, 2O "undetermined" via the trust [S6]. Moyer: valuation and the
   fulcrum; the plan of reorganization.
6. **Structural subordination and value outside the debtor.** Why: the
   flagship and the brands are not the noteholders' collateral. Fact:
   non-debtor flagship owner with a $1.25 billion CMBS; IP in
   non-guarantor subsidiaries licensed to the Authentic JV; HoldCo II's
   $200 million guarantee; Axonic pledge; marshaling clause [S1 ¶¶39,
   43, 46-47, 60][S4]. Moyer: structural versus contractual
   subordination; guarantees.

Supporting: **the committee's toolkit** (506(c), marshaling, challenge
period, critical vendors versus a litigation trust) [S4][S5][S6]; fold
into concepts 4 and 5.

## 12. Glossary

- **506(c) surcharge:** the estate's right to charge a secured creditor
  for the costs of preserving its collateral; DIP orders usually waive it.
- **ABL (asset-based loan):** a revolver whose availability is a
  percentage of eligible inventory and receivables (the borrowing base).
- **ABL Priority Collateral / Notes-Term Priority Collateral:** the two
  collateral pools in a split-lien structure; each lender group is first
  on one and second on the other.
- **Ad hoc group:** an informal group of holders organized to negotiate.
- **Adequate protection:** what a primed or diminished secured creditor
  receives in exchange.
- **Backstop premium:** a fee for committing to fund what others do not.
- **Challenge period:** the window in which the committee or others can
  contest the prepetition lenders' liens and claims.
- **CMBS:** commercial mortgage-backed securities; here, securitized
  mortgages on the flagship and the JV properties.
- **Creeping roll-up:** daily repayment of prepetition revolver loans
  with DIP loans as cash is swept.
- **Critical vendor:** a supplier the court allows the debtor to pay on
  prepetition claims because the business cannot run without it.
- **DIP (debtor-in-possession) financing:** post-petition loans with
  superpriority claims and usually priming liens.
- **DIP Conversion:** the plan step exchanging DIP claims for reorganized
  equity or take-back paper.
- **Disclosure statement:** the court-approved voting document with
  valuation, recoveries and risks.
- **Excess availability:** borrowing base minus drawings minus reserves.
- **FILO (first-in, last-out):** an ABL tranche repaid after the
  revolver; here an intercompany on-loan with first-out payment status.
- **First-out / second-out / third-out / fourth-out:** payment order
  among lenders sharing one lien.
- **Fulcrum security:** the most senior impaired class; receives the
  reorganized equity.
- **Intercreditor agreement:** the contract among lender groups fixing
  lien priority, payment order and remedies.
- **Liability management exercise (LME):** an out-of-court transaction
  that changes priorities or raises money without a filing.
- **Litigation trust:** a plan vehicle holding estate causes of action
  for a class of beneficiaries.
- **Marshaling:** requiring a creditor to recover from one asset pool
  before another.
- **Milestones:** dated case steps a DIP or RSA requires on pain of
  default or termination.
- **MOIC:** multiple of invested capital; a minimum return on the
  preferred units.
- **On-loan:** an intercompany loan passing bond proceeds from an SPV
  issuer to the operating company.
- **PIK (payment in kind):** interest or fees added to principal.
- **Priming lien:** a new lien ahead of existing liens on the same
  collateral.
- **Roll-up:** converting prepetition claims into DIP claims.
- **RSA (restructuring support agreement):** creditors' contract to
  support a plan on stated terms and milestones.
- **Springing maturity / guaranty:** a maturity or guarantee that
  activates on a trigger.
- **SPV (special purpose vehicle):** a subsidiary formed to issue debt or
  hold assets; here SGUS LLC.
- **Structural subordination:** a claim at a parent is paid only from
  what remains after the subsidiary's creditors; assets in non-debtor
  subsidiaries are outside the estate.
- **Stub rent:** rent for the part of the month after the petition date.
- **Take-back debt / preferred:** new paper issued under a plan to
  existing lenders in satisfaction of claims.
- **Term SOFR:** the floating benchmark that replaced LIBOR.
- **Unsecured creditors committee (UCC):** the court-appointed body for
  general unsecured creditors.
