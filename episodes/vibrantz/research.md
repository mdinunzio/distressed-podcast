# Vibrantz Technologies: a completed non-pro-rata LME — three funds get first-out paper at 98, everyone else gets 66 and 27, and the rating agency calls it a default for two weeks
As of: 2026-09-21. Slug: vibrantz. Deal type: completed out-of-court liability management exchange (private, non-pro-rata, with a first-out new-money tranche and tiered first-out / second-out / third-out exchange debt), closed February 17, 2026, with a second phase in March 2026; rated CCC with a negative outlook and negative free cash flow through the forecast, so the question now is redefault. Framing: live.

**Sourcing caveat.** Vibrantz Technologies Inc. (formerly PMHC II, Inc. / Prince International Corporation; Bloomberg ticker PRIMIN) is a private company owned by American Securities. It publishes no financial statements, and its loans and 9% notes were 144A/private instruments, so there is no EDGAR record of the issuer, no docket, and no public credit agreement, indenture or exchange memorandum. The primary documents that *are* public are: (a) the company's own IRS Form 8937 notice on the exchange, posted on vibrantz.com in April 2026, which gives the pre-exchange principal amounts, every exchange tranche's principal, and the issue price the company assigned to each tranche [S1]; (b) the company's, Cahill's and Finacity's press releases [S2][S3][S4]; (c) KeyBank's and Milbank's deal pages for the 2022 financing [S5][S6]; (d) a Barings BDC prospectus filed on EDGAR whose schedule of investments carries the Vibrantz term loan at a December 31, 2025 mark [S7]; (e) two S&P rating actions (March 5 and June 17, 2026) read through a reader proxy, which yielded the ratings, recovery ratings, participation percentages, leverage and cash-flow language but *not* the tranche sizes, coupons or the new-money amount [S8][S9]. The Bloomberg article on the side deals [S10], the Middle Market piece on private credit's response [S11], S&P's September 2025 and February 20, 2026 actions [S12][S13], Moody's actions [S14][S15], and the LCD/PitchBook default data [S16] were paywalled or blocked and are cited as search excerpts or partial page text, labelled as such. **Not sourced anywhere public**: the coupon, fees and exact size of the new-money first-out tranche (press says "about $350 million"), the size of the new revolver, 2025 revenue and EBITDA (only leverage multiples are public), the treatment of the roughly 1–3% of holdouts, and any post-June 2026 trading level. Where the research infers a figure from the Form 8937 arithmetic, it says so.

## 1. Situation summary and timeline

Vibrantz is a Houston-based specialty chemicals and materials company — pigments, colorants, glass and ceramic coatings, battery and electronics additives — created in April 2022 when American Securities' Prince International bought Ferro Corporation for about $2.1 billion and merged it with Chromaflo, financed with $2.77 billion of senior secured credit facilities and $756 million of 9% unsecured notes [S5][S6][S17]. Three and a half years later, with leverage at 11–12x, free cash flow negative and the rating in the CCC range, the company ran a private, two-track liability management exchange. Three funds — GoldenTree, Monarch and Oak Hill — were approached separately, put in about $350 million of first-out new money and exchanged their existing paper into first-out term loans that the company prices at 98 [S1][S10][S11]. Everyone else was offered second-out and third-out paper, on condition of signing a non-disclosure agreement and not organising against the deal, at issue prices of 66 and 27 cents on the dollar [S1][S10]. More than 90% took the deal in February, and after a second phase more than 99% of the term loan and about 97% of the notes had exchanged [S8][S9]. S&P cut the company to D on February 20, 2026, upgraded it to CCC two weeks later, and in June affirmed CCC with a negative outlook, forecasting 12–14x leverage and cash-flow deficits through its forecast [S9][S13]. Moody's rates it C [S15]. The trade is done; what is live is whether the runway it bought lasts, and what the lenders who were not in the room do about it.

## 2. Business overview and why it broke

Vibrantz describes itself as "a leading global provider of specialty chemicals and materials solutions": particle engineering, glass and ceramic science and colour technology, sold into batteries, electronics, agriculture, construction materials, paints and coatings, plastics, porcelain enamel and high-performance glass coatings. About 4,500 employees and 55 manufacturing sites on six continents (down from 63 sites at formation) [S4][S17][S22]. Its three legs: **Prince International** (American Securities' original platform: minerals and industrial additives for steel, glass, ceramics, batteries), **Ferro** (the NYSE-listed functional coatings and colour company Prince bought in 2022) and **Chromaflo** (liquid colourants and dispersions, another American Securities company folded in at the same time) [S5][S17]. S&P puts two-thirds of revenue in "building and construction, automotive, furniture, and home appliances" [S9] — the end markets that stalled in 2023–25.

**Causes of distress, in Moyer's categories.** (1) *Acquisition leverage, at the top of the cycle.* The 2022 deal loaded roughly $3.2 billion of funded debt (term loan plus notes) onto a company with about $2 billion of sales, priced when SOFR was near zero; the term loan floats at SOFR + 4.25%, so the 2022–23 rate rise alone added several hundred basis points of cash interest, and the Barings mark shows the loan paying 8.3% by late 2025 [S5][S7][S17]. (2) *A cyclical demand shock that did not reverse.* Press coverage of the September 2025 downgrade cites soft end-market demand, "prolonged customer de-stocking", industry overcapacity and volatile raw-material and energy costs; S&P in June 2026 adds inflation, tariffs and supply-chain problems, and expects 2026 EBITDA "marginally lower than 2025" [S9][S21]. (3) *Integration that took longer than the equity story assumed.* The founding CEO left in January 2025 declaring the "heavy lift of integration" done; the company then ran on an interim CEO and interim CFO for most of 2025, and the permanent team (King, Owenby, Bowlin, Gabel) arrived between November 2025 and July 2026 — after the credit had already broken [S18][S22][S23]. (4) *Negative free cash flow with a floating-rate structure*: S&P's line is that weak earnings and "high cash interest servicing costs" produce FOCF deficits, and that even after the exchange cash interest "modestly increases" [S8][S9]. This is Moyer's "leveraged balance sheet meets operating shortfall" case: the business is not broken, the capital structure is.

There is no public revenue or EBITDA figure for 2025. S&P's 11–12x end-2025 leverage against roughly $3.1 billion of pre-exchange funded debt (Form 8937 principal amounts: $2,360 million of term loan and $733 million of notes) implies EBITDA of about $260–280 million; the June 2026 12–14x on a similar debt quantum implies about $220–260 million. Both are this research's inferences from S&P's multiples and the notice's principal amounts, not reported numbers [S1][S8][S9].

**The accounting caveats a creditor applies.** With no financial statements, the creditor's numbers here come from the rating agency and from the market: S&P's leverage uses its own adjusted EBITDA, which is typically *lower* than the covenant "Adjusted EBITDA" the credit agreement lets the company report (add-backs for synergies and restructuring — the concept from episode 2). Fourth, S&P's CCC rating language in June is explicit that the exchange did not change the debt quantum: "total debt quantum largely unchanged" (March) [S8].

## 5. The key documents and what they say

None of the operative documents is public: not the 2022 credit agreement, the 9% notes indenture, the amendment that created the sub-tranches, the new intercreditor / first-out–second-out waterfall, the new revolver, or the exchange and NDA agreements. What can be read:

- **Form 8937 (April 2026)** [S1]. Its job is tax reporting, but it is the only document that lists every tranche. It says the exchanges "resulted in significant modifications" of the old debt (so, for tax, an exchange of old for new); that the A-2 tranche was "directly issued by an affiliate of the Company", a "separate and distinct taxpayer", so the side-deal exchange is a "fully taxable debt-for-debt exchange"; and that a "substantial amount" of A-2 was "sold for money" at $98 — i.e. the first-out group bought part of that tranche for cash at 98 and exchanged the rest at par. It states the issue prices the company will report for each tranche "based upon information available on pricing services": 66, 66, 27, 24, 66 and 24. Under the tax rules quoted in the notice, a publicly traded debt instrument's issue price is its trading value in the 31-day window around issuance — so these are, in effect, the market's marks on the new paper in the second half of February 2026. It also notes that the old term loan had a seven-year term and the notes eight to nine, while the new term loans have four to five and the new notes five to six — the "extended maturities" are one to three years of extension from 2029/2030 to 2030/2031.
- **The February 18, 2026 press release** [S2] and **Cahill's announcement** [S3]. "Over 90%" of first-lien term loan holders and unsecured bondholders, plus the revolving lenders; "new debt financing, extended debt maturities, and enhanced liquidity measures"; every holder "offered the opportunity to participate" — the sentence that matters legally, because it is the company's answer to the non-pro-rata charge: everyone could participate *in the second phase*, on second-out and third-out terms.
- **S&P, March 5 and June 17, 2026** [S8][S9]. The closest thing to a public term sheet: no scheduled amortisation on the new debt; maturities to 2030; new money; the tiered first-out / second-out / third-out first-lien structure; a new first-lien revolver; cash interest modestly higher.
- **Bloomberg, March 29, 2026** [S10] (partial text). The NDA condition, quoted above, and that some lenders "had little time to decide". Search excerpts of the same article and of The Middle Market's coverage [S11] carry the identification of GoldenTree, Monarch and Oak Hill as the separately approached group, the "about $350 million" first-out new money, and the phrase that they got "the biggest chunks of the highest-ranking debt".
- **The Barings 424B3** [S7]: the pre-exchange loan's terms (SOFR + 4.25%, 8.3%, 02/22–04/29) and its 55 mark.

What the documents *must* have contained, by inference from the outcome, and which the listener should know they cannot verify: an amendment to the 2022 credit agreement passed by the required lenders (a simple majority in a standard TLB) creating the A/B/C sub-tranches and the payment waterfall among them; a release or subordination of the holdouts' position, or at least a stripping of covenants, which is standard in these deals but not sourced here; and an intercreditor arrangement that puts the new revolver and the A tranches ahead of B and C in any enforcement.

## 6. Where the fulcrum security sits and why

With no EBITDA and no valuation, the fulcrum has to be read from prices and ratings rather than built up. Three things line up. First, S&P's recovery ratings: first-out at 1 (90–100%), second-out at 5 (10–30%), third-out at 6 (0–10%) [S9]. Second, the issue prices: 98 for first-out, 66 for second-out, 24–27 for third-out [S1]. Third, leverage of 12–14x against a specialty-chemicals peer set that trades at high-single-digit to low-double-digit EV/EBITDA multiples (a general market observation, not a Vibrantz-specific sourced figure; the listener should check the multiple against current comps). At a 9–10x multiple, enterprise value covers the revolver and the roughly $770 million of first-out paper several times over, covers *part* of the $1.5 billion of second-out, and leaves the $870 million of third-out and the equity with nothing. That is the Moyer fulcrum test — the class where value runs out — and it lands in the **second-out** tranches (B-1, B-2 and the Second Out Notes). The third-out paper at 24–27 is trading as a long-dated option on a recovery in earnings, and the sponsor's equity, which was not diluted in the exchange, is an option behind that option. The one caveat: this is an *out-of-court* structure, and the fulcrum only converts into ownership if the company files or does a second, equitising deal; until then the second-out holders own paper at 66, not the company.

## 7. The restructuring, step by step

1. **Autumn 2025: the setup.** S&P at CCC+ with 9–10x leverage and negative FCF; the loan in the 50s by year-end (Barings' 55 mark) [S7][S12]. Debtwire lists the name as a 2026 restructuring candidate; Gibson Dunn organises an ad hoc group of creditors [S20]. A new CEO arrives in November with a Pactiv Evergreen team [S22].
2. **The two tracks.** Per Bloomberg and The Middle Market (excerpts), the company and Kirkland approach GoldenTree, Monarch and Oak Hill *separately* from the rest of the lender group. They agree to provide about $350 million of new money in a first-out tranche (A-1) and to exchange $417 million of their existing loans and notes into a first-out A-2 tranche, issued by an affiliate and priced at 98 (the exchange ratio is not public; the notice gives only the old principal) [S1][S10][S11]. In exchange the company gets liquidity and, critically, a block of holders large enough that, combined with the ad hoc group, the amendment passes.
3. **The offer to everyone else.** The rest of the term-loan lenders and noteholders are shown the terms only after signing an NDA that also binds them not to talk to other lenders or challenge the deal before it closes, with "little time to decide" [S10]. Their menu: term-loan holders receive second-out B-1 plus third-out C-1 (per $100 of old loan, about $65 of B-1 and $26.5 of C-1: 91.7 of new face, worth about 50 at issue prices); noteholders receive B-2, C-2 and the new Second Out / Third Out Notes (per $100 of old notes, about $80 of new face, worth about 31 at issue prices) [S1]. These are this research's per-$100 calculations from the notice's aggregate amounts.
4. **February 17, 2026: closing.** More than 90% of each class participates [S2][S8]. Scheduled amortisation on the new term loans is eliminated; maturities move to 2030 (loans) and 2031 (new notes); a new first-lien revolver replaces the old one, with Cahill for the revolving lenders [S1][S3][S9].
5. **The rating cycle.** S&P: D on February 20 (a distressed exchange is a default under its criteria, because holders received less than promised and the company would otherwise have faced a conventional default), CCC/developing on March 5, CCC/negative with issue ratings on June 17 [S8][S9][S13]. Moody's: C on March 4 [S15]. Fitch's CLO data shows why the two-week D mattered: at 29% of Fitch-rated BSL CLOs, Vibrantz was the most widely held defaulted issuer of February, and a defaulted loan is haircut in CLO overcollateralisation tests where a CCC loan (within the CCC bucket) is carried at par — the point Rod Dubitsky's critique makes about the speed of the re-rating [S19][S25].
6. **March 2026: phase two.** The "vast majority" of the remaining noteholders exchange, taking participation to more than 99% of the loan and about 97% of the notes [S8][S9].
7. **Spring–summer 2026: the operating turnaround, on the new runway.** A CFO who was last a chief restructuring officer [S18]; a $200 million receivables facility that adds liquidity by carving receivables out of the lenders' collateral [S4]; S&P still forecasting cash burn [S9].

## 8. Who wins, who gets diluted or primed, and by how much

**The first-out group (GoldenTree, Monarch, Oak Hill, per press).** They moved $417 million of existing claims from a loan marked in the 50s into first-out paper priced at 98, took a $350 million new-money position rated B− with a recovery rating of 1, and in the process primed every other first-lien lender: the same collateral, now ranked behind them in the waterfall [S1][S9][S10]. If the A-2 exchange was at or near par — the notice gives no ratio, and press describes the group as getting "the biggest chunks of the highest-ranking debt" — a first-out participant holds close to 98 of value per $100 of old loan against about 50 for a second-track participant. That gap, whatever its exact size, is the price of being in the room.

**The exchanging term-loan lenders (mostly CLOs).** They gave up 8% of face and were moved from a single first-lien class to the second and third rungs of a three-tier first lien, behind about $770 million of first-out debt plus the revolver. Their paper: B-1 at 66 and C-1 at 27, blended about 50 on old par — roughly the loan's December mark, so the exchange crystallised the market's loss rather than adding to it, and bought a rating of CCC− on the bigger piece [S1][S7][S9].

**The exchanging noteholders.** From unsecured 9% notes due 2030 to a mix of second-out and mostly third-out paper worth about 31 cents on old par, but with a lien for the first time [S1]. Before the deal they were unsecured behind $2.4 billion of loans; after it they are secured but third-out behind $2.3 billion of first- and second-out debt. Whether that is better depends entirely on whether the enterprise is worth more than about $2.3 billion in a default.

**The holdouts (<1% of the loan, ~3% of the notes).** They keep their original coupons and 2029/2030 maturities, but the covenant and lien protection they keep is not public; in the standard version of this trade they are left with a stripped instrument behind the new structure [S1][S9].

**American Securities.** Kept 100% of the equity, got about $350 million of new liquidity and one to three years of extra maturity without a filing, and did not have to put in equity. The option is still alive. Air Methods, two years earlier, showed the alternative: a Chapter 11 in which the lenders took the company and the sponsor's equity was wiped out [S24].

**Where the value accumulated.** In the first-out tranches: $417 million of old claims exchanged into A-2 paper priced at 98, plus about $350 million of new money, secured first on the whole collateral package with a 90–100% recovery rating, created out of a loan that was trading at 55. Everything else in the structure was marked down in the exchange — the second-out at 66 and the third-out at 24–27 — and the enterprise value that backs those marks is the same as before the deal. No value was created; about $770 million of claims were moved to the front of the line and the rest were moved back.

**Twists, turns and risks.** The two-track approach with NDAs; the 48-point gap between tracks; the two-week D rating and its CLO consequences; the affiliate issuer for A-2; the second-phase mop-up of the notes; the negative outlook and continued cash burn after the deal; a receivables facility that takes collateral away from all three tiers; the unresolved position of the holdouts; and the sector-wide pushback (cooperation agreements, litigation) that Gibson Dunn's Scott Greenberg predicts for 2026 [S20][S26].

**Winners, losers, left in the dust.** Winners: the first-out group, and the sponsor for now. Losers: the second-out term-loan holders, who ate the market's loss and were primed to get a CCC− rating. Left in the dust: the third-out noteholders at 24, and any holdout with a stripped instrument behind $3 billion of newly senior debt.

## 10. How a special situations desk would look at each part of the structure

- **First-out A-1/A-2.** The trade that was only available to three funds. If any of it trades, it is the safest paper in the structure (B−, recovery 1) at a coupon that is not public; the desk question is whether the affiliate-issuer structure and the intercreditor hold up if a second-out group litigates, and whether the enterprise is worth the ~$770 million plus revolver ahead of everyone in a bad case — at 12–14x leverage, almost certainly yes [S1][S9].
- **Second-out B-1/B-2 and the Second Out Notes.** The fulcrum. At 66 the market is pricing partial recovery; the desk builds the enterprise value (needs an EBITDA estimate the public record does not give) and asks whether B is covered at a peer multiple. This is where a fund that wants to own the company in a second round would accumulate, and where a cooperation agreement among the CLO holders would form to stop a second non-pro-rata deal [S1][S9][S20].
- **Third-out C-1/C-2 and the Third Out Notes.** At 24–27, an option: worth something only if EBITDA recovers enough to push value through $2.3 billion of senior claims, or if a litigation claim against the exchange produces a settlement. Small size ($870 million), CC rated, 0–10% recovery rating [S1][S9].
- **The stub holdouts.** Illiquid, tiny, and only interesting as litigation claims: the question is what the amendment did to their rights [S9].
- **The revolver and the receivables facility.** The first money out in any scenario; the receivables facility, by taking receivables in 13 countries out of the collateral pool, reduces what the term lenders would recover in a liquidation — a desk marks the first-lien collateral coverage down for it [S4].
- **The equity.** Not tradeable, but the sponsor's incentives drive everything: with equity out of the money and no dilution taken, American Securities is long an option, and its rational move is to keep extending the runway. The desk models the *next* LME, not just this one [S17][S26].
- **Relative value.** The loan's 55 mark in December was roughly the blended value of B-1 plus C-1 at issue (50); the exchange was priced at the market. The interesting historical trade was buying the loan in the 50s in late 2025 *if* you were going to be invited into the first-out track — and the whole controversy is that only three funds were.

## 11. Risks and what could derail it

1. **Redefault.** S&P's own triggers: persistent FOCF deficits, liquidity sources below 1x uses, a missed interest payment, or "another distressed exchange" [S9]. Cash interest went *up*; the new-money runway and the receivables facility are finite; Dubitsky's point that LMEs "typically have high redefault rates" is a widely reported industry pattern [S25].
2. **Litigation over the non-pro-rata structure.** The NDA-and-deadline process, the affiliate issuer for A-2 and the "everyone was offered the chance to participate" defence are the ingredients of the lender-on-lender disputes of 2022–25; no suit is public, but Greenberg's prediction is more litigation and more cooperation agreements in 2026 [S10][S20][S26].
3. **The second-out is where the next fight happens.** If earnings do not recover, a second deal would need the B holders, and this time they will be organised.
4. **End markets.** Two-thirds of revenue in construction, automotive, furniture and appliances; tariffs and inflation named by S&P [S9].
5. **Collateral leakage.** Receivables now financed outside the lien; any further carve-out (a drop-down of a subsidiary, an unrestricted-subsidiary transfer) would follow the episode 2 and 3 patterns [S4].
6. **Information.** A private company with no reporting: lenders see numbers, the market does not; the public record here is thinner than in any prior episode, and the hosts should say so.

## 13. Glossary

- **Non-pro-rata exchange**: a debt exchange in which holders of the same instrument are offered different terms, typically better terms to a group that provides new money or votes.
- **First-out / second-out / third-out**: tiers within a single lien class that fix the order in which proceeds of the shared collateral are paid.
- **New-money tranche**: debt issued for cash, usually at the top of the structure, as part of a restructuring.
- **Distressed exchange**: an exchange in which holders receive less than the original promise and which the issuer would otherwise be unable to honour; rating agencies treat it as a default (S&P "D"/"SD").
- **Issue price**: for US tax, the first price at which a substantial amount of a new debt instrument is sold for money or, for publicly traded debt, its trading value around issuance; reported by issuers on Form 8937.
- **Form 8937**: the IRS report of organisational actions affecting the basis of securities, which an issuer must publish after an exchange.
- **CLO (collateralised loan obligation)**: a securitisation vehicle that holds a portfolio of leveraged loans and issues rated notes against it, subject to tests that limit CCC-rated and defaulted holdings.
- **CCC bucket**: the portion (usually 7.5%) of a CLO's portfolio that may be rated CCC and still be carried at par in its coverage tests.
- **Cooperation agreement (co-op)**: a contract among lenders to negotiate and act together and not to accept a side deal.
- **NDA / going restricted**: agreeing to receive material non-public information under confidentiality, which restricts the holder from trading and, here, from talking to other lenders.
- **Recovery rating**: S&P's scale (1+ to 6) for expected recovery of principal in a default; 1 = 90–100%, 5 = 10–30%, 6 = 0–10%.
- **Dual-track default rate**: LCD's default measure that counts distressed exchanges as well as payment defaults.
- **Distress ratio**: the share of a loan index trading below 80 cents on the dollar.
- **Amortisation**: scheduled repayment of principal before maturity; the old TLB paid about 1% a year, the new loans none.
- **Affiliate issuer**: a company related to the borrower that issues debt, here the A-2 first-out loans.
- **Receivables financing facility**: a facility secured by, or funded by the sale of, trade receivables, taking them out of the general collateral.
- **Chief restructuring officer (CRO)**: an executive appointed to run a balance-sheet restructuring, the role the new CFO held at his previous company.
- **Interest coverage**: EBITDA (or EBIT) divided by interest expense.
- **Free operating cash flow (FOCF)**: S&P's term for cash from operations less capital expenditure.
- **Redefault**: a second default by a company that has already restructured.
